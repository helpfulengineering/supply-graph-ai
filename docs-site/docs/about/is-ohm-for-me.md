# Is OHM for you?

OHM is useful to different people for genuinely different reasons — not variations
on one reason. Find yourself below.

Whoever you are, the first step is the same and costs nothing: **look for your
workshop on the map.** Several thousand spaces are already there, drawn from
directories that already existed. There's a reasonable chance you're one of them.

## University labs and FabLabs

**The strongest fit today.**

Universities and their fab labs produce an enormous amount of open hardware that
effectively disappears. A project is built, documented on a course page or a lab
wiki, and then becomes unfindable to the lab next door, let alone to a partner
institution three countries away.

OHM gives that output a single discoverable form. It can read existing projects
from where they already live, so backfilling a back catalogue doesn't mean
re-documenting years of work by hand. And because OHM is open-source software you
can run yourself, a consortium can operate its own instances and share between
them without anything passing through a third party.

There's a smaller, more everyday benefit too. When a student needs a machine that
their own lab's is broken or busy or the wrong size, finding another one that
fits — inside a group of institutions that already trust each other — is currently
near-impossible. It shouldn't be.

## Community workshops and makerspaces

Three things, in rough order of how soon they matter.

**Being findable in your region.** People near you are looking for the things you
can make, and mostly they have no way to discover you.

**Mutual aid, when it's needed.** You don't have to be a disaster-response
organisation for this to apply. In a crisis, the constraint is rarely willingness
— it's knowing who can make what, where, quickly. That network can't be built
during the emergency. Being findable in ordinary times *is* the preparation.

**Describing yourself once, usefully.** Most spaces already publish something
about their equipment somewhere. OHM's value depends on that description being
good — and being good takes your time, which is a real cost. We know that. It's
our job to make it worth it, not yours to do us a favour.

## Organisations that need to run their own

If you work somewhere that cannot put its data on someone else's infrastructure —
a public institution, a research body, an NGO with a duty of care to the people
in its records — this is for you.

OHM is designed to be self-hosted. You run it, in your jurisdiction, under your
control, and connect it to whichever other instances you choose and none that you
don't. The version we host is a convenience for people who don't want to run
software. It is not the product, and it is not a hub that anything has to pass
through.

See [federation and sovereignty](federation-and-sovereignty.md) for what that
means in practice.

## Designers and engineers

If you've published an open hardware design, or are about to, OHM is how someone
who isn't you can find out that they could build it.

That is closer to the point of open hardware than it might sound. A design only
its author can manufacture is open in licence and closed in practice.

## Commercial manufacturers

Honestly: **not yet.**

If you're a prototyping shop or a small manufacturer whose interest is commercial
work, OHM doesn't currently offer you enough. There's no order flow, no
quoting, no payment. You'd be listing your capabilities into a network that isn't
yet sending you business.

We'd rather say that plainly than have you find out in a fortnight. The pieces
that would change it are real and planned — see
[what's built and what isn't](../reference/whats-built.md) — and if it's the kind
of thing you'd want to be told about when it arrives, get in touch.

## Still not sure?

Read [what OHM is not](what-ohm-is-not.md). It's often faster to rule something
out than to rule it in.
